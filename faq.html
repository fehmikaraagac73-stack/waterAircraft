THE CONCEPT THE VESSELS ENGINEERING COMPLIANCE & SAFETY BUYING & DELIVERY PROGRAM & COMPANY

The short version, if you read nothing else. No ANUNNAKI vessel has been built. No airframe has been acquired, no classification society appointed, and no plans submitted for approval. Every figure on this site is a design target. Everything below is written on that basis.

01

The concept

Is this a serious engineering proposal or an art project?

A serious proposal, and the site is built so you can check that. The general arrangement drawings on each vessel page carry dimensions, frame stations, watertight subdivision and machinery locations, and the compliance page sets out which instruments apply. It has not been built, and we say so on every page.

Why an aircraft fuselage rather than building a deckhouse?

It arrives as a complete, already-engineered aluminium structure with published structural data and a maintenance history, so a naval architect can analyse it rather than guess. It is also cheap, because the alternative buyer is a scrap merchant, and the resulting profile has commercial value for tourism and charter operators. The counter-argument is that a purpose-built deckhouse has fewer faying surfaces to detail against corrosion — that trade is discussed on the technology page.

Does the aircraft still fly, or could it?

No, and it never will again. The wings, engines, landing gear and wing box are removed and returned to the parts market. What remains is a fuselage shell used as superstructure. Nothing about the conversion is reversible, and we do not return airframes to flight.

Which aircraft type do you use?

All three configurations are built on a single Boeing 747 fuselage, wings removed and tail assembly retained. That fixes length overall at 70.6 m and gives the internal envelope the cargo configuration depends on. Smaller airframes are a separate study — none of the figures on this site apply to them.

Where does the name come from?

It is a Sumerian term, chosen for the older meaning — something between the sky and the water. We are aware it has been thoroughly adopted by ancient-astronaut literature and that this dominates the search results. The response is to make the site more technical, not to hope nobody notices. There is a fuller answer on the about page.

02

The vessels

What is actually different between the three configurations?

Length, beam and structural arrangement are common to all three. What changes is deck layout, propulsion sizing, draft, and the annex the vessel is registered under — 3.6 m draft and 2 × 2,500 kW on the passenger vessel, 3.8 m and 2 × 2,200 kW on the cargo barge, 3.5 m and 2 × 1,800 kW hybrid on the yacht. The comparison sheet puts all three against one schema.

How many passengers can it really carry?

The 450 figure on the passenger page is an arrangement study, not a certified capacity. Certified numbers come out of the damage stability calculation and the egress analysis, in that order, and they can only go down from an arrangement figure — never up. That is why it is labelled a target throughout.

Will it fit our berth?

Three numbers answer most berth questions: length overall 70.6 m, beam overall 22.4 m, draft 3.5–3.8 m depending on configuration. Beam is the one that rules the vessel out — 22.4 m is wide for the length, because pontoon separation is what provides the stability, and it makes a meaningful number of berths, locks and channels unusable. Check it before anything else.

What can the cargo version actually carry?

Up to 120 tonnes across roughly 1,500 m² of enclosed volume, with a clear internal envelope of about 6.1 m by 3.0 m. Loading is via the retained side cargo door, an aft ramp, the open deck, or an optional 5 t davit. Bay tonnages on the cargo page are a nominal distribution; actual permitted loading comes from the trim and stability booklet, issued per vessel.

Can the interior be customised?

Extensively, within one constraint: bulkhead positions are set by the original fuselage frame stations, where the structure is already sized to take concentrated load. Moving them becomes a structural change rather than a fit-out change, and is priced accordingly. On the yacht that is why cabin count is quoted as a range — twelve at standard frame pitch, ten if two are combined into a full-beam suite.

03

Engineering

What actually keeps it afloat — the fuselage or the pontoons?

The pontoons, entirely. They carry the full loaded displacement, all the machinery, all the tankage and both propulsion trains; the fuselage contributes no buoyancy and is not credited with any in the calculation. The separation is deliberate — it means the class society is assessing a conventionally engineered marine hull with an unusually shaped superstructure on top.

Is a fuselage strong enough to be used this way?

It is being asked to do less than it was designed for. In flight it hangs from its wing box and is pressurised from inside; afloat it is supported from below at discrete points with no pressure differential. Reinforcement is added where the original structure was never intended for this — the saddle interfaces, the closed wing-root aperture, and the aft crown opening on the yacht. The load-path diagram is on the technology page.

Will aluminium survive in salt water?

The fuselage never enters the water — it sits above deck level, and aluminium superstructures are conventional in shipbuilding. What it faces is salt air and spray, a coating and detailing problem rather than a material one. The specific risks are galvanic coupling at the fuselage-to-deck interface and crevice corrosion at faying surfaces, both addressed with isolation, sealant and a monitoring regime written into the survey cycle.

How does it behave in a seaway?

Unverified — no tank testing or sea trials have been carried out, so this is reasoning from the hull form rather than measurement. A wide twin hull is comfortable in short seas and stable at rest, which suits ferry work and lying alongside as a venue. It is less comfortable in a long beam swell, where the roll period can become unpleasant, which is the argument for stabilisation on the yacht configuration.

Which propulsion option should we take?

It follows from the route. Diesel-electric is the default for passenger and cargo work; hybrid suits the yacht and premium ferries where silent operation at anchor matters; twin mechanical diesel is the cost-led option for cargo. Full electric is viable only on short protected-water routes returning to a berth with high-capacity charging — for a 70 m vessel on an open crossing it is not currently practical. The four are compared on the technology page.

04

Compliance & safety

Short answers here. The full regulatory route, the applicable instruments by configuration, and the detailed safety questions live on the compliance page.

Is it certified?

No. No classification society has been appointed, no plans have been submitted for approval, and no flag administration has been engaged. The intended seven-step route, from concept design through plan approval, survey, inclining experiment and flag registration, is set out on the compliance page.

Which rules does a vessel like this fall under?

The ordinary ones: SOLAS, the Load Line Convention, the applicable intact stability code, MARPOL and COLREG. US operation additionally requires USCG inspection under 46 CFR Subchapter T, and the yacht configuration also targets the Large Yacht Code. Where prescriptive rules do not map cleanly onto an unusual arrangement, the route is an equivalent-safety case agreed with the society — which takes longer and costs more, and is the largest single risk in the program.

What happens if a compartment floods?

Each pontoon is divided into six watertight compartments, and the fuselage above into five zones serving as both fire and watertight boundaries. A single breach floods a limited volume, and the opposite pontoon's reserve buoyancy resists the resulting heel — the righting arm is large because the hulls are far apart. Residual heel and surviving freeboard are calculated per damage case at plan approval; until then the honest description is modelled, not compliant.

How do 450 people get off in an emergency?

Two independent escape routes from every enclosed space, marked exits on both sides along the full fuselage length, muster stations on the open promenade deck, and eight 50-person life rafts mounted four per side. The egress analysis is also one of the two calculations that sets the final certified passenger number.

05

Buying & delivery

What does one cost?

We cannot quote a price before a yard is appointed. What we can show is the cost structure, on the program page. The useful thing to know early is that the airframe is the cheap part — the value and the risk both sit in the engineering and the certification route.

How long would delivery take?

Unknown, and we will not commit to a date before an airframe is secured and a yard appointed. If you need a vessel in service within 24 months, we are not the right supplier yet — and would rather tell you now than at contract.

Can we buy one now?

Not yet. Phase 1 is a single demonstration vessel intended to produce measured data, not a saleable ship, and commercial sales are gated on Phase 2. Early conversations are still worth having — route, berth constraints and cargo or passenger mix change the design before anything is drawn.

Who operates and maintains it?

You or your operator. We convert, deliver, and handle crew familiarisation; we do not operate vessels. Maintenance follows the class survey cycle — annual, intermediate and special surveys — plus the additional corrosion monitoring an aluminium superstructure in salt air warrants.

06

Program & company

What is the current status?

Phase 1, design study, as of June 2026. No airframe acquired, no hull laid, no society appointed, no trials conducted. The program page states this in more detail and is dated so you can tell how stale it is.

What has to happen before this is real?

One demonstration vessel that proves four things: flotation against calculation, propulsion and manoeuvring, stability via an inclining experiment, and the customer experience of boarding and circulation. Budget estimate USD 2M. The inclining experiment is the point at which target particulars become actual particulars.

What is most likely to go wrong?

Certification, by a distance — a first-of-type has no precedent for a society to reason from. Then weight growth, since marine outfit is heavier than aviation outfit for the same volume, and growth in lightship weight costs draft and speed. After that: corrosion over service life, berth compatibility at 22.4 m beam, and finding a yard with both aluminium aerostructure and marine hull experience. The full register is on the program page.

Is this actually more sustainable, or is that marketing?

One claim is defensible without modelling: a converted airframe is one fewer fuselage entering the scrap stream. Beyond that we make no claims — we have not commissioned a lifecycle assessment, the vessel is not carbon neutral, the solar array does not propel it, and we do not know whether it beats a comparable ferry until the power curves are measured. The sustainability page lists what we will not say and why.

Why does the site use drawings instead of photographs?

There is nothing to photograph yet, and earlier generated renders carried artefacts — garbled lettering, an inconsistent logo, and in one case a yacht depicted as a business jet when the specification described a 747. Clean renders will be commissioned from the arrangement drawings before any public launch.
