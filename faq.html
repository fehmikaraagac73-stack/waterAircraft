THE CONCEPT THE VESSELS ENGINEERING COMPLIANCE & SAFETY BUYING & DELIVERY PROGRAM & COMPANY

The short version, if you read nothing else. No ANUNNAKI vessel has been built. No airframe has been acquired, no classification society appointed, and no plans submitted for approval. Every figure on this site is a design target. Everything below is written on that basis.

01 · The concept

The concept

Is this a serious engineering proposal or an art project?

A serious proposal, and the site is deliberately built to let you check that rather than take our word for it. The general arrangement drawings on each vessel page carry dimensions, frame stations, watertight subdivision and machinery locations. The compliance page sets out which instruments apply.

It has not been built, and we say so on every page. That is the difference between an unproven proposal and an unserious one.

Why an aircraft fuselage rather than building a deckhouse?

Three reasons, in order of how much they matter. It arrives as a complete, already-engineered aluminium structure with published structural data and a full maintenance history — a naval architect can analyse it instead of guessing. It is cheap, because the alternative buyer is a scrap merchant. And it is unmistakable from three miles away, which for tourism and charter operators is the product.

The counter-argument is honest too: a purpose-built deckhouse has fewer faying surfaces and fasteners to detail against corrosion. That trade is discussed on the technology page.

Does the aircraft still fly, or could it?

No, and it never will again. The wings, engines, landing gear and wing box are removed and returned to the parts market. What remains is a fuselage shell used as superstructure. Nothing about the conversion is reversible, and we do not return airframes to flight.

Which aircraft type do you use?

All three current configurations are built on a single Boeing 747 fuselage, wings removed and tail assembly retained. That fixes length overall at 70.6 m and gives the internal envelope the cargo configuration depends on.

Smaller airframes are a separate study with entirely different particulars. If you have seen ANUNNAKI material showing a yacht on a regional or business jet fuselage, none of the figures on this site apply to it — see the note on the yacht page.

Where does the name come from?

It is a Sumerian term, chosen for the older meaning — something between the sky and the water. We are aware it has been thoroughly adopted by ancient-astronaut literature and that this dominates the search results. The response is to make the site more technical, not to hope nobody notices. There is a fuller answer on the about page.

02 · The vessels

The vessels

What is actually different between the three configurations?

Length, beam and structural arrangement are common to all three. What changes is deck layout, propulsion sizing, draft, and the annex the vessel is registered under — 3.6 m draft and 2 × 2,500 kW on the passenger vessel, 3.8 m and 2 × 2,200 kW on the cargo barge, 3.5 m and 2 × 1,800 kW hybrid on the yacht.

The comparison sheet puts all the particulars against one schema so you can read the differences directly.

How many passengers can it really carry?

The 450 figure on the passenger page is an arrangement study, not a certified capacity. Certified numbers come out of the damage stability calculation and the egress analysis, in that order, and they can only go down from an arrangement figure — never up.

Anyone quoting a capacity for an unbuilt, unapproved hull as though it were a specification is overselling. That is why it is labelled a target throughout.

Will it fit our berth?

Three numbers answer most berth questions immediately: length overall 70.6 m, beam overall 22.4 m, draft 3.5–3.8 m depending on configuration.

Beam is the one that rules the vessel out. 22.4 m is wide for the length, because the pontoon separation is what provides the stability, and it makes a meaningful number of berths, locks and channels unusable. It is worth checking before anything else.

What can the cargo version actually carry?

Up to 120 tonnes across roughly 1,500 m² of enclosed volume, with a clear internal envelope of about 6.1 m by 3.0 m — wider than the internal width of a 40-foot container and taller than a high cube. Loading is via the retained side cargo door, an aft ramp, the open deck, or an optional 5 t davit.

Bay tonnages shown on the cargo page are a nominal distribution. Actual permitted loading comes from the longitudinal strength calculation and the trim and stability booklet, issued per vessel.

Can the interior be customised?

Extensively, within one constraint: bulkhead positions are set by the original fuselage frame stations, because that is where the structure is already sized to take concentrated load. Moving them is possible but it becomes a structural change rather than a fit-out change, and it is priced accordingly.

On the yacht that is why cabin count is quoted as a range — twelve at standard frame pitch, ten if two are combined into a second full-beam suite.

03 · Engineering

Engineering

What actually keeps it afloat — the fuselage or the pontoons?

The pontoons, entirely. They carry the full loaded displacement, all the machinery, all the tankage and both propulsion trains. The fuselage contributes no buoyancy in the intact condition and is not credited with any in the calculation.

This separation is deliberate. It means the class society is assessing a conventionally engineered marine hull, with an unusually shaped superstructure sitting on top of it.

Is a fuselage strong enough to be used this way?

It is being asked to do less than it was designed for. In flight it hangs from its wing box and is pressurised from inside; afloat it is supported from below at discrete points with no pressure differential. The second load case is the easier one.

Reinforcement is added where the original structure was never intended for this: the cradle saddle interfaces, the closed wing-root aperture, and the aft crown opening on the yacht. The load-path diagram is on the technology page.

Will aluminium survive in salt water?

The fuselage never enters the water — it sits above deck level, and the pontoons are separately engineered marine structures. What it faces is salt air and spray, which is a coating and detailing problem rather than a material one. Aluminium superstructures are entirely conventional in shipbuilding.

The specific risks are galvanic coupling at the fuselage-to-deck interface and crevice corrosion at faying surfaces, both addressed with isolation, sealant and a monitoring regime written into the survey cycle.

How does it behave in a seaway?

Unverified — no tank testing or sea trials have been carried out, so anything here is reasoning from the hull form rather than measurement.

A wide twin hull is comfortable in short seas and stable at rest, which is what suits it to ferry work and to lying alongside as a venue. It is less comfortable in a long beam swell, where the roll period can become unpleasant. That is a genuine characteristic of the form, and it is the argument for fin or gyro stabilisation on the yacht configuration.

Which propulsion option should we take?

It follows from the route, not from preference. Diesel-electric is the default for passenger and cargo work. Hybrid suits the yacht and premium ferries where silent operation at anchor or in a marina matters. Twin mechanical diesel is the cost-led option for cargo.

Full electric is real only for short protected-water routes returning to a berth with high-capacity charging. For a 70 m vessel on an open crossing it is not currently practical and we would rather say so than sell it. The four are compared on the technology page.

04 · Compliance & safety

Compliance & safety

Short answers here. The full regulatory route, the applicable instruments by configuration, and the detailed safety questions live on the compliance page.

Is it certified?

No. No classification society has been appointed, no plans have been submitted for approval, and no flag administration has been engaged. Anyone telling you otherwise about an ANUNNAKI vessel is wrong.

The intended seven-step route, from concept design through plan approval, survey, inclining experiment and flag registration, is set out in full on the compliance page.

Which rules does a vessel like this fall under?

The ordinary ones. SOLAS, the Load Line Convention, the applicable intact stability code, MARPOL and COLREG. US operation additionally requires USCG inspection under 46 CFR Subchapter T. The yacht configuration also targets the Large Yacht Code.

A novel hull form does not get a novel rulebook. Where prescriptive rules do not map cleanly onto an unusual arrangement, the route is an equivalent-safety case agreed with the society — which takes longer and costs more, and is the largest single risk in the program.

What happens if a compartment floods?

Each pontoon is divided into six watertight compartments, and the fuselage above into five zones serving as both fire and watertight boundaries. A single breach floods a limited volume, and the opposite pontoon's reserve buoyancy resists the resulting heel directly — the righting arm is large precisely because the hulls are far apart.

Residual heel and surviving freeboard are calculated per damage case at plan approval. Until that calculation is done and reviewed, the honest description is “modelled and plausible”, not “compliant”.

How do 450 people get off in an emergency?

Two independent escape routes from every enclosed space, marked exits on both sides along the full fuselage length, muster stations on the open promenade deck, and eight 50-person life rafts mounted four per side.

The egress analysis is also one of the two calculations that sets the final certified passenger number, alongside damage stability.

05 · Buying & delivery

Buying & delivery

What does one cost?

We cannot quote a price, and any number given before a yard is appointed would be fiction. What we can show is the cost structure — which lines dominate and which are trivial — on the program page.

The one useful thing to know early: the airframe is the cheap part. The value and the risk both sit in the engineering and the certification route.

How long would delivery take?

Unknown, and we will not commit to a date before an airframe is secured and a yard appointed. If you need a vessel in service within 24 months, we are not the right supplier yet — and would rather tell you now than at contract.

Can we buy one now?

Not yet. Phase 1 is a single demonstration vessel intended to produce measured data, not a saleable ship. Commercial sales are gated on Phase 2, which is gated on Phase 1 completing.

Early conversations are still worth having. Route, berth constraints and cargo or passenger mix change the design before anything is drawn, and first customers shape a first-of-type more than later ones do.

Who operates and maintains it?

You or your operator. We convert, deliver, and handle crew familiarisation; we do not operate vessels. Maintenance follows the class survey cycle — annual, intermediate and special surveys — plus the additional corrosion monitoring an aluminium superstructure in salt air warrants.

06 · Program & company

Program & company

What is the current status?

Phase 1, design study, as of June 2026. No airframe acquired, no hull laid, no society appointed, no trials conducted. The program page states this in more detail and is dated so you can tell how stale it is.

What has to happen before this is real?

One demonstration vessel that proves four things: flotation against calculation, propulsion and manoeuvring, stability via an inclining experiment, and the customer experience of boarding and circulation. Budget estimate USD 2M.

The inclining experiment is the single most important event in the program. It is the point at which target particulars become actual particulars.

What is most likely to go wrong?

Certification, by a distance — a first-of-type has no precedent for a society to reason from. After that: weight growth, since marine outfit is heavier than aviation outfit for the same volume, and if lightship weight grows then draft grows and speed falls.

Then corrosion over service life, berth compatibility at 22.4 m beam, finding a yard with both aluminium aerostructure and marine hull experience, and whether novelty that sells the first hull still sells the tenth. The full register is on the program page.

Is this actually more sustainable, or is that marketing?

One claim is defensible without any modelling: a converted airframe is one fewer fuselage entering the scrap stream, and the structure keeps doing structural work instead of being melted into billet.

Beyond that we make no claims. We have not commissioned a lifecycle assessment, the vessel is not carbon neutral, the solar array does not propel it, and we do not know whether it beats a comparable ferry until the power curves are measured. The sustainability page lists what we will not say and why.

Why does the site use drawings instead of photographs?

Because there is nothing to photograph yet, and because earlier generated renders carried artefacts — garbled lettering, an inconsistent logo, and in one case a yacht depicted as a business jet when the specification described a 747.

The audience most likely to notice those problems is exactly the audience this site has to convince. Clean renders will be commissioned from the arrangement drawings before any public launch. Until then, lines and dimensions.
