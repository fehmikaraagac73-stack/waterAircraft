Position statement

Narrow claims, or none.

Reusing a retired airframe is a genuine and checkable benefit. It is also a smaller benefit than the marketing instinct wants it to be, and overstating it is the fastest way to lose a technical reader.

What is true

An airframe that becomes a vessel is an airframe that is not scrapped.

A retired widebody is dismantled for the parts with resale value — engines, avionics, landing gear, control surfaces — and the remaining airframe structure is cut up and sold as aluminium scrap. Recycling rates are good; the material genuinely re-enters the supply chain. But scrapping still consumes energy in cutting, transport and re-smelting, and the structure's engineering value is entirely lost.

Reuse keeps the structure intact. The frames, stringers and skin continue doing structural work for another service life instead of being melted into billet. That is the real claim, and it is defensible without any lifecycle modelling: one fewer fuselage enters the scrap stream per conversion.

It also displaces the material that would otherwise have been fabricated new to build a deckhouse of similar volume. That displacement is real but harder to quantify, because it depends on what the comparison vessel would have been.

What we will not claim

Four statements you will not find on this site.

Each of these is a claim similar projects make. Each would need evidence we do not have.

NOT CLAIMED

"Carbon neutral"

A diesel-electric vessel burning up to 80,000 L of fuel is not carbon neutral, and no amount of airframe reuse changes the operating emissions. The hybrid and electric options reduce them; nothing here eliminates them.

NOT CLAIMED

A specific tonnage of CO₂ avoided

We have not commissioned a lifecycle assessment. Publishing a number without one would be inventing it. If a customer requires an LCA, we will commission it for that project and publish the methodology alongside the result.

NOT CLAIMED

"Solar powered"

The yacht's 400 m² array covers a meaningful share of hotel load at anchor in good conditions. It does not propel a 70-metre vessel, and describing it as solar powered would be false.

NOT CLAIMED

Lower emissions than a comparable ferry

Unknown until the resistance and power curves are measured. A wide twin hull has different drag characteristics from a monohull, and whether it comes out ahead depends on speed and loading. We will publish the measured figures.

Operating profile

Where the emissions reductions actually come from.

Three levers, in order of how much difference each makes.

Propulsion selection

The largest lever by a wide margin. Choosing hybrid over mechanical diesel, or full electric on a route that supports it, changes operating emissions by more than every other measure on this page combined.

Shore power at berth

A vessel alongside running generators for hotel load is a large share of port emissions. All three configurations carry a shore-power interface; whether it gets used depends on the berth, which is a customer decision.

Solar at anchor

Real but modest, and only on the yacht configuration where the crown array is fitted. Sized against hotel load at anchor, not against propulsion.

Service speed discipline

Resistance rises steeply with speed. Operating at 14 kn rather than 18 kn is the cheapest emissions reduction available and requires no equipment at all — only a schedule that permits it.

End of life

The vessel's own retirement.

A conversion that produces an un-recyclable object at the end of its service life has moved the problem rather than solved it. Both the pontoon hulls and the fuselage remain aluminium structures with a normal scrap route, and the design does not use composite bonding or mixed-material construction that would compromise that.

Interior fit-out is the part most likely to be replaced mid-life. Specifying it for removal and reuse — mechanical fixing rather than adhesive, standard panel sizes — is a low-cost decision at build that pays back at the first refit.

Nothing on this page has been independently verified. These are design intentions and reasoned positions. They become claims only when a built vessel and a commissioned assessment support them.
