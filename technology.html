Conversion engineering

Turning a pressure vessel into a superstructure.

Five engineering problems separate an airframe from a ship: preparation, load transfer, buoyancy, subdivision, and propulsion. This page describes how each is addressed.

01 · Fuselage preparation

Strip, survey, treat, seal.

The most common failure mode for aluminium in salt air is not fatigue. It is corrosion at joints and fasteners, and it is entirely preventable at build time.

Everything not structural comes out: seats, monuments, insulation blankets, wiring looms, hydraulics, ducting, the whole cabin fit. What remains is skin, frames, stringers, longerons and floor beams, which is the part with published structural data.

A corrosion survey follows — the airframe's own maintenance history tells us where to look, and the areas that matter for a marine life are the lower lobe, the galley and lav surrounds, and any location where a previous repair introduced dissimilar metals.

Treatment is conversion coating and marine-grade primer over the full internal surface, with sealant at every faying surface and fastener. External finish is a marine topcoat system, not an aviation livery system — different flexibility, different service life, different reapplication interval.

Openings are then addressed. Door apertures are re-hung with marine seals; window stations are either retained, enlarged where the frame pitch permits, or plated over. Anything below the eventual deck line is closed permanently.

02 · Structural reinforcement

The load case is upside down.

In flight, a fuselage hangs from its wing box and is pressurised from inside. Afloat, it is supported from below at discrete points and carries no pressure differential at all. Those are different problems, and the second one is easier.

The fuselage is cradled at saddles positioned at original station locations, where the frames are already sized to take concentrated load. Each saddle spreads into a transverse deck girder that spans the full beam to both pontoons. That is the entire primary load path: fuselage weight down through saddles into girders, buoyancy up through the pontoons into the same girders.

Reinforcement is added where the original structure was never designed for this — the saddle interfaces themselves, the aft crown opening on the yacht configuration, and the deck edge where the removed wing root leaves an unusually large aperture that has to be closed and re-stiffened.

The wing box itself is removed with the wings. It is the heaviest single structure in the airframe and it serves no purpose once the vessel's loads are carried by the deck.

03 · Pontoon hull integration

All the ship parts live in the hulls.

The pontoons are not floats bolted to a fuselage. They are the vessel; the fuselage is what sits on top of it.

Each pontoon is a conventionally engineered marine hull with its own frames, watertight transverse bulkheads and double bottom. Together they carry the full displacement, all the machinery, all the tankage and both propulsion trains. That separation is deliberate: the marine survey and class case concerns a structure a class society already knows how to assess, and the fuselage above it is a superstructure with an unusual shape.

Hull separation is the parameter that fixes almost everything else. Wide separation produces a large transverse metacentric height, which is what makes a tall, light superstructure sit still. It also fixes beam overall at 22.4 m, which fixes which berths, locks and channels the vessel can use — and that is a commercial constraint as much as an engineering one.

The bow of each pontoon is raked and fine to reduce resistance; the stern is squared to carry the propulsion pods and, on the yacht, the swim platform between them.

ADisplacement

Both pontoons carry the full loaded displacement. The fuselage contributes buoyancy only in a damaged condition, and is not credited in the intact case.

BMachinery spaces

Gen sets, switchboards, and drive trains sit low in the pontoons, keeping the vertical centre of gravity down.

CTankage

Fuel, fresh water, grey and black water, and ballast, distributed between the two hulls and trimmed against each other.

DDouble bottom

Grounding protection along the full length, with the double bottom void space used for ballast and trim.

04 · Buoyancy, stability, subdivision

What happens when a compartment floods.

The question every regulator and every insurer asks first. The answer has to be arithmetic, not reassurance.

Each pontoon is divided into six watertight compartments by transverse bulkheads. The fuselage above is divided into five zones which serve double duty as fire zones and as watertight boundaries above the deck. A single flooded compartment in one pontoon produces a heel that the opposite pontoon's reserve buoyancy resists directly — the righting arm is large precisely because the hulls are far apart.

The intact stability case is straightforward for a wide twin hull. The damage case is where the work is, and it is the calculation that will determine final permitted passenger numbers on the cruise liner — which is why the capacity figure on that page is written as a target rather than a specification.

An inclining experiment on the Phase 1 vessel is what converts all of the above from calculation into measured fact. Until that happens, the honest description of the stability position is: modelled, plausible, unverified.

Not yet validated. No tank testing, resistance testing or inclining experiment has been carried out. The subdivision arrangement shown is a design intent drawing, not an approved damage stability plan.

Systems

Everything else that has to be aboard.

NAV

Navigation & communication

Radar, AIS, ECDIS, GNSS, VHF/DSC and satellite communication. The mast arrangement is carried on the retained vertical stabiliser, which puts the antennas high without adding a separate structure.

FIRE

Fire protection

Detection throughout, sprinkler coverage in accommodation and service spaces, fixed suppression in machinery spaces, and structural fire boundaries at the five zone divisions.

HVAC

Climate & ventilation

A fuselage in the tropics is a solar collector. Marine HVAC sized for the glazed area, with insulation replacing the aviation blankets that were stripped at preparation.

ELEC

Electrical distribution

Marine standard distribution — the aircraft's 400 Hz architecture is removed entirely. Shore-power connection on all configurations for quiet berthing.

WATER

Fresh & waste water

Fresh water tankage and, on the yacht, a watermaker. Grey and black water holding to MARPOL discharge requirements, with capacity sized to the route's port intervals.

MOOR

Mooring & anchoring

Anchor arrangement forward of each pontoon, with windlasses and a mooring deck at each corner. Twin hulls give four clean corners, which is easier than a monohull of the same length.
