Regulatory route

A novel hull does not get a novel rulebook.

Everything about this vessel is unusual except the requirements it has to satisfy. Those are the same ones every other passenger vessel of this size and service satisfies, and the whole design is arranged to sit inside them.

The route

From design study to a vessel with a flag.

Seven steps. We are at step one, and the honest position on each of the rest is stated below.

No approvals have been obtained. ANUNNAKI has not appointed a classification society, submitted plans for approval, or engaged a flag administration. The route below is the intended path, not a record of progress.

  1. STEP 01 · CURRENT

    Concept design & general arrangement

    Principal dimensions, arrangement, and preliminary weight estimate. The drawings on the vessel pages are the output of this step.

  2. STEP 02

    Class society appointment

    Engage a recognised organisation and agree the applicable rule set for a novel hull form, including any risk-based or equivalent-safety approach where prescriptive rules do not map cleanly.

  3. STEP 03

    Plan approval

    Structural drawings, subdivision and damage stability, fire safety plan, machinery and electrical arrangements submitted for review. This is where the fuselage-as- superstructure argument is either accepted or reworked.

  4. STEP 04

    Survey during construction

    Attendance at material receipt, welding and joining, watertight boundary testing and installation of safety systems.

  5. STEP 05

    Inclining experiment & sea trials

    Measured lightship weight and centre of gravity, then speed, manoeuvring, endurance and noise trials. The point at which target particulars become actual particulars.

  6. STEP 06

    Flag registration

    Registration with a flag administration, issue of statutory certificates, and tonnage measurement. US operation additionally requires USCG inspection under 46 CFR Subchapter T.

  7. STEP 07

    Operation & periodic survey

    Annual, intermediate and special surveys on the class cycle, plus the additional corrosion monitoring regime an aluminium superstructure in salt air warrants.

Safety systems

Designed in, not added afterwards.

01Watertight subdivision

Six compartments per pontoon, five zones through the fuselage. Damage stability calculated per compartment, not assumed.

02Fire detection

Smoke and heat detection across all enclosed spaces, with a bridge-mounted indicating panel and zone addressing.

03Sprinkler coverage

Accommodation and service spaces sprinklered; fixed suppression in machinery spaces within the pontoons.

04Means of escape

Two independent escape routes from every enclosed space, marked exits both sides throughout the fuselage length.

05Life-saving appliances

Eight 50-person life rafts on the passenger configuration, mounted both sides with muster stations on the open promenade.

06Structural fire boundaries

Zone divisions serve as both watertight and fire boundaries, using insulated marine bulkhead construction rather than aviation partitions.

07Emergency power

Independent emergency generator and battery supply above the waterline, feeding navigation, lighting and communication.

08Bilge & flooding detection

Level detection in every watertight compartment reporting to the bridge, with cross-connected bilge pumping.

Questions we actually get

The hard ones first.

Is an aircraft fuselage strong enough to be a ship?

It is not being asked to be a ship. The pontoon hulls are the ship — they carry the displacement, the machinery and the class case. The fuselage is superstructure sitting on a cradle, and superstructure loads are far lower than the flight loads the airframe was designed for.

The reinforcement work that is genuinely required is at the interfaces: the cradle saddles, the closed wing root aperture, and any large opening cut into the crown.

Will aluminium survive in salt water?

The fuselage never enters the water — it sits above the deck, and the pontoons are separately engineered marine structures. What the aluminium faces is salt air and spray, which is a coating and detailing problem rather than a material problem. Aluminium superstructures are entirely conventional in shipbuilding.

The specific risks are galvanic coupling at the fuselage-to-deck interface and crevice corrosion at faying surfaces. Both are addressed with isolation, sealant and a monitoring regime written into the survey cycle.

Has a classification society approved this?

No. No society has been appointed and no plans have been submitted. That is step two of the route above and it has not been started. Anyone telling you otherwise about an ANUNNAKI vessel is wrong.

Where do the 70.6 m and 450 passenger figures come from?

Length overall follows from the 747 fuselage envelope plus the pontoon extension aft. Beam follows from the hull separation chosen for stability. Those two are reasonably firm because they are geometry.

The 450 figure is an arrangement study, not a certified capacity. Certified passenger numbers come out of the damage stability calculation and the egress analysis, and they can only go down from an arrangement figure, never up.

What happens if a pontoon compartment floods?

The opposite pontoon's reserve buoyancy resists the resulting heel, and because the hulls are widely separated the righting arm is large. Each pontoon is divided into six watertight compartments so that a single breach floods a limited volume.

The residual heel angle and the surviving freeboard are calculated per damage case as part of plan approval. Until that calculation is done and reviewed, the correct description is "modelled and plausible", not "compliant".

Can it operate in open ocean?

The intended service is protected and near-coastal water — ferry routes, island supply, and coastal cruising. Service area is a flag and class restriction stated on the vessel's certificates, and for a first-of-type it will be conservative.

A wide twin hull is comfortable in short seas and less so in a long beam swell, where the roll period can become uncomfortable. That is a real characteristic of the hull form and it argues for stabilisation on the yacht configuration.

Is the airframe's history a liability?

It is the opposite of a liability in one specific way: the airframe arrives with a complete, auditable maintenance and repair record. A naval architect assessing a second-hand steel hull rarely has anything comparable.

The selection criteria are corrosion survey results, repair history and frame-station integrity. Airframes that fail those are not candidates, and there are enough retired 747s that we can be selective.

What is the current build status?

Phase 1 — design study and prototype planning. No hull has been laid, no airframe acquired, no sea trials conducted. The program page states this in more detail and is dated.
